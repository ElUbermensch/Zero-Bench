Two apps, one account

A log and a bench, on the same database.

Most shooters keep two records that never speak to each other: a data book at the range and a loading log at home. The whole point of keeping both is the line between them — which load shot that score, in what air.

Zero — at the range

The data book

Shots plotted on a real target face, scored on the ring they touch. Sessions carry position, distance, wind, temperature and light. Matches, courses of fire, classification pace. Pair fire, so a coach's phone and a shooter's phone show the same string as it is fired.

Bench — at home

The loading room

Components, recipes, batches, brass and its firing history. Every round that leaves the bench is attached to a batch, so when a string goes into Zero the count comes back down and the batch knows what it has left.

One account, one sync. Both install to the home screen and both keep working with the phone in airplane mode, which is the state a phone is usually in at a match.

The feature

A solver that trues to your DOPE.

Every ballistic app asks for a muzzle velocity and a BC and gives you a curve. Both numbers are usually wrong — the box velocity is measured from a longer barrel, and a published G1 BC is a marketing number that varies with the speed it was measured at. The curve is wrong by an amount you cannot see at 200 and cannot miss at 1000.

Zero starts from the other end. It already holds your confirmed zeros, so it uses them: it runs a G7 drag integration and searches for the velocity and drag scale whose trajectory passes through the distances you have actually fired. The rifle it finds is the rifle you own.

This is the difference between fitting a curve and fitting a rifle. A polynomial through three points also passes through three points, and then diverges everywhere else — which is exactly the failure a shooter cannot detect until they are on the line at 1000 with no time to diagnose it. A drag model constrained by physics cannot do that; the only freedom it has is which rifle it is describing.

It refuses

One zero is not a solution

Velocity moves the near end of the trajectory and drag moves the far end. Raise one and lower the other and the curve passes through the same single point. One anchor constrains neither, so the solver declines and says so, rather than returning a number that looks like an answer.

It widens

An interval, not a decimal

Between your anchors the prediction is interpolation and the interval is tight — ±0.15 MOA, floored there because a confirmed zero was read off paper by a person. Past the furthest anchor it is extrapolation and the interval grows with the distance: ±1.38 MOA at 1000 off a 600 yd anchor.

It flags

Transonic is marked

Around Mach 1.2 a G7 curve is least trustworthy and a real bullet can go unstable in ways no drag model describes. Predictions in that band are labelled, and the point where your load goes subsonic is shown, because past it the number is a guess wearing a decimal point.

And the part nobody solves for you

Positions have their own zero.

Prone, sitting and standing do not shoot to the same place, and the difference is yours — it comes from your cheek weld, your sling tension and how much the rifle cants when your elbow is not under it. No table has that number.

So Zero learns it. Where you have fired two positions at the same distance and the same range, it pairs them, takes the difference in elevation and windage, and averages it across every pair it has. It reports the spread alongside it, so a single strange relay is visible rather than absorbed. A position with no partner at a distance contributes nothing — it is not extrapolated into existence.

The library

Forty‑two NRA faces, transcribed from the rule book.

Every ring diameter is typed from the published dimensions, not derived from a reduction formula. The formula is close and it is wrong: it puts the B‑19's rings at 1.80, 3.60, 5.40, 7.20 inches where the book says 1.78, 3.58, 5.38, 7.18. Four hundredths of an inch is the difference between a 10 and a 9 on a scored shot.

Pin the ones you shoot.

A High Power shooter needs three faces and will never open the other thirty‑nine. Pinned targets sit at the top of the picker, in your order, saved to your account and not to the device — so it follows you to the phone in your range bag. Everything else stays one scroll away.

Rectangles score correctly.

Zone targets with rectangular or offset scoring areas used to be measured against a bounding circle, which meant a shot could come back up to eleven inches from where it was fired. The boundary is now solved along the shot's own bearing, so a shot placed on paper and read back off paper lands in the same hole.

What's inside

The rest of it.

Pair fire

Two phones, one string

A coach and a shooter join the same relay. Shots appear on both faces as they are called, numbered once and only once, and a second device cannot take a live shooter's role out from under them.

Offline

Signal is optional

Both apps are installable and both work with no network. Everything queues; the queue survives being closed, being killed, and being reopened three days later on a different network.

Sync

Every device, one record

Changes walk up and down on a keyset cursor, so a page boundary cannot skip a row or replay one forever. A row the server refuses is held and can be sent again rather than silently dropped.

Conditions

Air is part of the record

Temperature, pressure altitude and light are kept with the string, and the solver uses air density — thin summer air needs about 3.7 MOA less at 1000 than dense winter air on the same load.

Brass

A case knows its life

Firings, trim length, annealing, and which batch it went into. When a batch is fired in Zero, the count comes back to Bench without anybody typing it twice.

Matches

Courses of fire

Build a match from a template, shoot it stage by stage, and keep the aggregate. Classification pace is computed against your record rather than estimated.

Straight about it

What it does not do.

Written down here rather than discovered on the line.

It does not call the wind
There is no wind solver. Wind is recorded with the string and it is part of what you review afterwards, but the number on the knob is still yours. A wind call from an app that cannot see the flags is theatre.
Two zeros, 100 yd apart
Below that the solver will not true, because those anchors cannot separate velocity from drag. It says so rather than fitting something. One confirmed zero gets you a curve from the box numbers, marked as such.
The B‑27 series is absent
Its scoring rings are ovals, and the geometry engine works in circles, rectangles and polygons. A B‑27 drawn with round rings would score wrong at the 9 and 8 line, so it is not in the library rather than in it and lying.
Bench syncs one direction
Firearms, sessions and groups come down to every device. Component lots, recipes, batches and brass go up but not yet down — each needs a defensible merge before it can, and cloud backup covers moving a bench to a new device meanwhile.
Transonic is a caution
Past about Mach 1.2 the drag model is doing its worst work and a real bullet may do something no model predicts. The prediction is still shown, marked, with a wider interval. Confirm it on paper.

About

Built by somebody who shoots it.

Zero and Bench exist because the two records that matter most to a precision shooter — what the rifle did, and what was in the round that did it — are almost always kept in two places that cannot be joined. A spiral notebook in a range bag and a spreadsheet at home cannot tell you that the group that opened up at 600 was the batch you loaded with the new lot of powder. One database can.

The design rule is that the app never invents a number. A score comes from the ring the shot touched. A round count comes from a batch that was actually fired. A come-up comes from a drag model fitted to zeros you confirmed, with an interval on it, and when the data cannot support a solution the app says so instead of producing one. That rule is why the solver refuses single anchors and why the B‑27 is missing.

It is held to that by test rather than by intention: 1,019 assertions across the geometry, the solver, the sync engine, the relay and both apps, plus a separate suite that runs against the database's own access rules. Every fix carries a test that fails without it. That is the only way to know a fix is a fix.
