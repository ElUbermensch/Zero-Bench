Two apps, one account

A data book and a loading room, on the same database.

Most shooters keep two records that never speak to each other: a notebook in the range bag and a spreadsheet at home. The whole value of keeping both is the line between them — which load shot that score, in what air, out of brass on its fifth firing.

Zero — at the range

The data book

Shots placed on a real target face and scored on the ring they touch. Sessions carry position, distance, wind, temperature and light. Matches and courses of fire, DOPE, a trajectory solver fitted to your own zeros, and pair fire so a coach’s phone and a shooter’s phone show the same string as it is fired.

Bench — at home

The loading room

Component lots, cited recipes, loaded batches, and brass with its whole history — firings, trim, annealing, culls. Stock is derived from what your batches actually drew, never typed. Labels with a QR code back to the record.

Both install to the home screen. Both keep working with the phone in airplane mode, which is the state a phone is usually in at a match.

Inside Zero

What it records, and what it works out.

Everything below except pair fire runs on the device, with no account and no signal.

The shot

Hold, call, impact

Tap where the sights were when the trigger broke, then where the round actually went. The gap between the two is the shot you did not know you threw — and it is scored, tracked and charted over a season.

The string

Scored as a scorer would

Score and X count on the ring each hole touches. Extreme spread and mean radius in MOA and inches, with a confidence band, because a five-shot group is a small sample and the app says how small.

The diagnosis

Dispersion or position

Points lost to the group being big, split from points lost to it sitting off centre. That is the difference between needing to shoot better and needing to move your sights, and it is the first thing worth knowing after a string.

DOPE

Filed the way you shoot

Confirmed zeros by firearm, then range, then distance and position — recorded from the sight setting you finished each string on. Drift warnings when a zero moves. A printable card sized to tape to a stock.

Conditions

Wind, called and scored

Speed, direction on a clock face, and the hold you predicted. Over time the app tells you how good your wind calls actually are, and whether you are biased in one direction.

Trends

Analytics that answer something

Does your first cold shot go somewhere different? When you dial a correction, does the group move by what you dialled — and how often does it move the wrong way? Group size by position and distance, with a flag for whether a change is real or inside the noise.

Matches

Courses of fire, prebuilt

Pick the National Match Course or a Regional / XTC and every stage is created in firing order with its target, distance, position and fire mode set. Shoot the day and fill in the shots. Classification pace is computed against your record.

Pair fire

Two phones, one string

A four-character code puts a partner and a coach on the same relay. Each shooter’s string draws over the other’s target in their own colour, with a feed for wind calls. No account needed to join.

Targets

Forty-two NRA faces

High Power, Smallbore, Pistol, F-Class and Long Range, with every ring diameter transcribed from the rule book. Pin the three you shoot to the top of the picker and draw your own for steel.

Inside Bench

What you have, what you made, what it has been through.

Bench records no shots of its own — those belong to the app where the trigger was pulled. It records everything that led up to them.

Inventory

Lots, not products

Bullets, powder and primers by lot number, with cost and vendor. Remaining stock is derived from what your batches drew, not typed, so it cannot drift. Powder reports how many more rounds of a given recipe it will make.

Recipes

A cited source is mandatory

Cartridge, components, charge, COAL, CBTO — and where the data came from, which is a required field. The app records load data. It never suggests, interpolates or extrapolates a charge, and it ships with none.

Batches

It refuses to over-draw

A live preview shows exactly what comes off the shelf as you type the round count. Short on primers and it names the lot, the shortfall, and how many rounds you can actually make. Cartridge mismatches and worn-out brass are refused outright.

Brass

A case knows its life

Firings, trim length, annealing with a nag interval, weight-sort band, cost amortised per round. Cull three split cases and the survivors’ firing average is untouched — they have been fired exactly as often as they had been a moment ago.

Identify

Which lot is this case?

Tap the colours you can see on the case head and neck, leave the ones you cannot as unknown, and it narrows to the matching lots while drawing the case. Or scan the serial off a label.

Workup

A ladder, side by side

Every batch on one recipe against the axis you actually varied, with velocity, SD, ES, group and pressure signs per rung. It points out the flattest step and is explicit that this is an observation, not a recommendation.

The feature

A solver that trues to your own DOPE.

Every ballistics app asks for a muzzle velocity and a BC and gives you a curve. Both numbers are usually wrong — box velocity is measured from a longer barrel than yours, and a published G1 BC is a marketing figure that varies with the speed it was measured at. The curve is wrong by an amount you cannot see at 200 and cannot miss at 1000.

Zero starts from the other end. It already holds your confirmed zeros, so it uses them: it runs a G7 drag integration and searches for the velocity and drag scale whose trajectory passes through the distances you have actually fired. The rifle it finds is the rifle you own.

This is the difference between fitting a curve and fitting a rifle. A polynomial through three points also passes through three points, and then diverges everywhere else — which is exactly the failure a shooter cannot detect until they are on the line at 1000 with no time to diagnose it. A drag model constrained by physics cannot do that; the only freedom it has is which rifle it is describing.

It refuses

One zero is not a solution

Velocity moves the near end of the trajectory and drag moves the far end. Raise one and lower the other and the curve passes through the same single point. One anchor constrains neither, so the solver declines and says so, rather than returning a number that looks like an answer.

It widens

An interval, not a decimal

Between your anchors the prediction is interpolation and the interval is tight. Past the furthest one it is extrapolation, the interval grows with the distance, and the table says how far past your furthest zero each row is reaching.

It flags

Transonic is marked

Around Mach 1.2 a G7 curve is least trustworthy and a real bullet can go unstable in ways no drag model describes. Predictions in that band are labelled, and the point where your load goes subsonic is shown, because past it the number is a guess wearing a decimal point.

And the part nobody solves for you

Positions have their own zero.

Prone, sitting and standing do not shoot to the same place, and the difference is yours — it comes from your cheek weld, your sling tension and how much the rifle cants when your elbow is not under it. No table has that number.

So Zero learns it. Where you have fired two positions at the same distance and the same range, it pairs them, takes the difference in elevation and windage, and averages it across every pair it has. It reports the spread alongside, so a single strange relay is visible rather than absorbed. A position with no partner at a distance contributes nothing — it is not extrapolated into existence.

What it needs from you: two confirmed zeros at least a hundred yards apart, on the same firearm, in the same position, at the same range. A confirmed zero is not something you mark — it is the sight setting you finished a string on. Dial your sights on screen as you dial them on the rifle and you are already recording them. Together

The join the two apps exist for.

Load a batch in Bench. Shoot it in Zero. The session, the group and every individual shot cross over, Bench works out what is left, and the count comes back — without anybody typing a number twice. Then the batch’s own screen draws the target and the whole string, in the order it was fired.

That is the question neither half can answer alone: this group, from this batch, from these component lots, on brass at this firing.

Getting started

Five steps, then you are logging.

No setup wizard, and nothing here needs an account or a signal — except importing a batch from Bench in step two, which needs both. If you keep a data book already this is the same sequence, with the arithmetic done for you.

  1. Add your firearm. Name and chambering. Barrel life if you track it.
  2. Add the load you shoot — or import the batch from Bench, which brings its chronographed velocity and BC across with it.
  3. Create a session. Target, distance, position, and the name of the range, which is part of how your DOPE is filed.
  4. Log the string. Tap each shot where it went, and dial your sights on screen as you dial them on the rifle.
  5. Read it back. Score, group, centre and diagnosis are already computed.

About

Built by somebody who shoots it.

Zero and Bench exist because the two records that matter most to a precision shooter — what the rifle did, and what was in the round that did it — are almost always kept in two places that cannot be joined. A notebook in a range bag and a spreadsheet at home cannot tell you that the group which opened up at 600 was the batch loaded with the new lot of powder. One database can.

The design rule is that the app never invents a number. A score comes from the ring the shot touched. A round count comes from a batch that was actually fired. A come-up comes from a drag model fitted to zeros you confirmed, with an interval on it, and when the data cannot support a solution the app says so instead of producing one. That rule is why the solver refuses single anchors, why a recipe cannot be saved without citing its source, and why the B‑27 is missing from the target library.

It is held to that by test rather than by intention: 1,256 assertions across the geometry, the solver, the sync engine, the relay and both apps, plus a separate suite that runs against the database’s own access rules. Every fix carries a test that fails without it. That is the only way to know a fix is a fix.

Before you ask

The honest limits.

Written down here rather than discovered on the line.

It does not call the wind
There is no wind solver. Wind is recorded with the string, your predicted hold is scored against your impacts, and you get told how good your calls are — but the number on the knob is still yours. A wind call from an app that cannot see the flags is theatre.
Two zeros, 100 yd apart
Below that the solver will not true, because those anchors cannot separate velocity from drag. It says so rather than fitting something. With less than that you get the load’s own numbers, marked as a starting point rather than as your rifle.
Only linked sessions cross
A Zero session reaches Bench only if its load is linked to a Bench batch, with at least two record shots and a distance. A session shot with factory ammo stays on your device — fully logged and fully analysed, just not shared with the bench.
Most of Bench syncs one way
Firearms move both ways, shot data flows to Bench, and batches come to Zero when you import them. Component lots, recipes and brass go up but do not come back down — each needs a defensible merge rule first. Cloud backup covers moving a whole bench to a new device meanwhile.
A cloud restore merges
It adds what the backup has and this device does not. It never deletes or overwrites, which means an edit made on one device does not reach the other by restoring. To make two devices match exactly there is a separate replace, behind two confirmations.
The B‑27 series is absent
Its scoring rings are ovals, and the geometry engine works in circles, rectangles and polygons. A B‑27 drawn with round rings would score wrong at the 9 and 8 line, so it is not in the library rather than in it and lying.
Transonic is a caution
Past about Mach 1.2 the drag model is doing its worst work and a real bullet may do something no model predicts. The prediction is still shown, marked, with a wider interval. Confirm it on paper.
